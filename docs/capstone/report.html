    ControlSift: Reducing Inequality in Evidence Assurance with Small-Model Triage Research

    Mentor Me Collective · Google Cloud Launchpad · DeepMind AI Research Foundations Capstone
    MMC rubric option: Goal 4 — Reduced Inequalities · Official UN designation: SDG 10
    Dataset v1.1.0 · Protocol protocol-v1-locked · Solo project · Primary metric: macro F1 · Seed 42

    1. Problem statement and objectives

    Cybersecurity assurance depends on evidence that controls operate as intended. A policy that requires MFA is relevant to an access control but does not, by itself, prove that privileged accounts were enrolled during the audit period. NIST SP 800-53A Rev. 5 similarly frames control assessment around verifying implementation, control objectives, and desired outcomes rather than document presence alone.

    Organizations with limited cybersecurity budgets also face uneven access to specialist capacity. NIST's Small Business Cybersecurity Corner explicitly notes limited resources and budgets and the difficulty some organizations have affording dedicated cybersecurity staff. ControlSift studies a narrow response to that access problem: whether an openly reproducible small-model method can assist human evidence triage without lowering the assurance standard.

    Objective: Determine whether QLoRA adaptation of Gemma 3 1B Instruct improves five-class evidence-quality classification (SUFFICIENT, PARTIAL, INSUFFICIENT, IRRELEVANT, CONTRADICTORY) on a sealed synthetic benchmark. Scope: research and education only; no production auditor, no private customer evidence, and human judgment remains authoritative.

    2. Research and analysis

    External grounding combines standards, public-sector guidance, and primary technical literature. NIST SP 800-53A Rev. 5 grounds the control-assessment problem; NIST small-business guidance documents real cybersecurity resource constraints; Dettmers et al.'s QLoRA paper grounds the memory-efficient fine-tuning method; Google DeepMind's Gemma 3 model documentation grounds the lightweight 1B model choice; and the NIST AI RMF grounds explicit scope, oversight, and human-accountability boundaries. These sources motivate the problem and method; they do not establish ControlSift's performance. See annotated research sources.

    Key sources: NIST SP 800-53A Rev. 5 · NIST Small Business Cybersecurity · QLoRA · Gemma 3 Model Card · NIST AI RMF Core.

    Modeling data is synthetic by design: 1,500 compositional evidence packets, family-isolated splits (1000/200/200/100), rule-derived labels with structural audit and a scripted spot-check. An early draft allowed TF-IDF to saturate; dataset v1.1 was hardened so lexical shortcuts no longer defined a trivial ceiling. The evaluation protocol was sealed before final large-model claims.

    Sealed TEST macro F1: majority 0.0667; TF-IDF 0.5327 (challenge 0.5237); Gemma zero-shot 0.0796; few-shot 0.1365; QLoRA 0.0827 with label parse success about 0.435. TF-IDF leads; few-shot is the best Gemma rung; QLoRA does not beat few-shot.

    3. Solution development

    Solution ladder: majority → TF-IDF → Gemma zero-shot → few-shot → Gemma 3 1B + QLoRA (4-bit NF4). The public research artifact includes machine-readable results, Failure Lab, Data Card, Model Card, AI risk register, intended-use boundaries, curriculum map, and reproducibility instructions.

    Innovation: the project treats evidence quality as a compositional assurance problem, not generic document relevance. Practicality and sustainability: the benchmark is synthetic, the classical baseline runs cheaply, and the language-model experiment uses a lightweight model and free/commodity GPU infrastructure. The open method can scale as a research and training artifact; production use would require real-world validation, stronger constrained output handling, security review, and accountable human oversight.

    Finding: the proposed AI adaptation is not the winner. The inexpensive classical method performs substantially better on the sealed benchmark. Keeping that result is part of the project's responsible-AI contribution.

    4. Implementation plan and risk management

    Phases 0–4 are complete: problem framing and SDG mapping; benchmark generation and hardening; protocol seal; classical baselines; Gemma prompted baselines; QLoRA training/evaluation; error analysis; public site; report and slides packaging. Final administrative phase: record the ≤5-minute video and upload the required files.

    Resources included Python, scikit-learn, Gemma 3 1B, Hugging Face access, Kaggle/Colab free GPU paths, GitHub Pages, and the public repository. Risks addressed include dataset leakage, lexical shortcuts, prompt tuning on the test set, fabricated metrics, secret exposure, synthetic-to-real overgeneralization, weak model output parsing, and users mistaking research triage for automated audit. Mitigations include family-isolated splits, a protocol seal, CI checks, null-until-run metrics, platform secrets, explicit limitations, Failure Lab publication, and mandatory human-review boundaries.

    5. Outcomes and recommendations

    • Published a sealed benchmark and receipts for all five model-ladder experiments.
    • Hardened an initially trivial dataset until the lexical baseline fell from saturation to about 0.53 macro F1.
    • Produced an honest negative result: QLoRA < few-shot, and all Gemma rungs < TF-IDF.
    • Published failure analysis and assurance artifacts rather than reporting only aggregate scores.
    • Demonstrated a low-cost, reproducible research path while retaining explicit limits on real-world use.

    Recommendation: do not deploy ControlSift as an audit autopilot. Preserve human authority; improve constrained label generation before further language-model claims; validate on carefully governed real-world evidence before discussing operational use; and continue reporting the full ladder, including when classical methods win.

    6. Claim boundaries and authorship

    External literature supports context and technical choices; ControlSift-specific result claims come only from sealed experiment files. Labels are rule-derived on synthetic data, not a large human gold standard. Assistive tools were used for drafting and engineering support; problem selection, experimental design, protocol decisions, benchmark decisions, execution, interpretation, and final submission responsibility remain with the author.
